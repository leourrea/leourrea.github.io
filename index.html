About

Leonardo Urrea R.

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Economics at the University of Pittsburgh (expected 2027). I study public finance and macroeconomics in developing economies: how governments adjust when debt becomes unsustainable, how fiscal rules perform in practice, and who bears the cost of fiscal and economic crises. Most of my work uses Colombian administrative and fiscal data.

My job market paper, Fiscal Adjustment without Collapse, studies close to a hundred Colombian departments and municipalities that restructured their debts under Law 550 without a bailout, and asks who paid for the adjustment.

Before the Ph.D. I spent a decade working on fiscal policy from the inside: as a macroeconomic analyst at Colombia's Ministry of Finance, as a researcher and consultant at the Inter-American Development Bank, and as an advisor to the Caribbean Development Bank. I have taught economics at Universidad Nacional de Colombia, Universidad de los Andes, Universidad del Rosario and the University of Pittsburgh.

Research

Research

I work at the intersection of public finance and macroeconomics, with a focus on how governments in developing economies manage debt, fiscal rules and crises. I combine newly coded administrative records with modern panel methods, and I draw on a decade of work inside fiscal institutions to choose the questions.

Job Market Paper

Fiscal Adjustment without Collapse: Restructuring Subnational Debt under Colombia's Law 550

with Gabriel Tourek (UC Davis)

When a subnational government becomes insolvent, the usual options are a bailout or a forced adjustment that cuts its core functions. We study a third option: Colombia's Law 550, under which nearly a hundred departments and municipalities restructured their debts and restored fiscal sustainability. They did so through agreements with creditors that set both the debt terms and the fiscal measures, without a bailout from the central government. We code every agreement between 2000 and 2016, match it to the Ministry of Finance's creditor ledger, and compare signers with governments that never entered, using synthetic difference-in-differences under a single monetary policy. Treated municipalities paid a quarter less interest, and their overall balance improved by five points of revenue without a large primary surplus. Payroll and capital spending each fell by a sixth, social investment rose, taxes did not change, and local output did not contract. Creditors were paid the recognized debt at face value.

Working Papers

Who Dies in a Crisis? Economic Collapse and Violent Death in Colombia, 1998–2004

with Juliana Jaramillo Echeverri and Christian Posso

In advanced economies, fewer people die during economic downturns. In Colombia's crisis of 1999, its worst recession of the twentieth century, the death rate at working ages rose, and homicide accounts for all of the rise. We ask whether the crisis caused these deaths. We combine 1.4 million death certificates of the urban residents of 1,014 towns with a measure of how hard the crisis hit each town: the shortfall of its night lights below their own trend. Where an armed group operated before the crisis, a town one standard deviation further short has 14.3 log points more homicides in 2001–04 than a neighbor in the same department (standard error 4.8), about 11 more per 100,000 residents a year. We find no difference between them in the five years before the crisis. The added victims are working-age men killed with firearms. Kidnappings rise in the same towns, and no other cause of death responds. The shortfall accounts for about 900 homicides a year in 2001–04 and, over 1999–2002, for between a fifth and a third of the rise in homicide.

Español

En español

Soy candidato a doctor en Economía de la Universidad de Pittsburgh. Investigo finanzas públicas y macroeconomía en economías en desarrollo: cómo se ajustan los gobiernos cuando su deuda deja de ser sostenible, qué tan bien funcionan las reglas fiscales en la práctica y quién asume los costos de las crisis fiscales y económicas. Buena parte de mi trabajo usa datos fiscales y administrativos de Colombia.

Antes del doctorado trabajé diez años en política fiscal: como analista macroeconómico e investigador del Ministerio de Hacienda y Crédito Público (2012–2018), en el Banco Interamericano de Desarrollo y como asesor del Banco de Desarrollo del Caribe. He sido profesor en la Universidad Nacional de Colombia (2014–2022), la Universidad de los Andes, la Universidad del Rosario, la Universidad de La Salle y la Universidad de Pittsburgh.
